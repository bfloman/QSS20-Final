A switch, not a dial.

Political science usually treats legislative power as a yes-or-no question: which party holds the majority. This project asks whether the size of that majority matters too. Does a party with 70 percent of the seats govern differently from one with 51 percent? The test case is Medicaid. After the 2014 expansion, a state could tighten the program with work requirements, premiums, and enrollment caps, or leave it alone. Using monthly data on 49 states from 2015 to 2026, the project sorts between two pictures: a dial, where bigger majorities restrict more, or a switch, where only holding control matters.

The answer: which party controls the legislature decides whether a state restricts Medicaid. How large the majority is does not.

+ The question

Veto-player theory says a bigger majority should be able to act more, and to do so continuously: more seats, more policy. That is a dial.

The alternative is a switch. For some policies, what matters is simply holding the majority, and extra seats add nothing once you have it. Filing for a restrictive Medicaid waiver is plausibly that kind of action: it takes control of the chamber to do, but a bare majority and a supermajority can file the same request. The paper sets these two predictions against each other.

+ The data and method

Four public sources, merged to the state-month: Medicaid and CHIP enrollment (Kaiser Family Foundation), the poverty denominator (Census ACS), legislative composition (NCSL), and restrictive Section 1115 waivers (CMS). The result is a panel of 4,118 state-months across 49 states.

One feature of the data shapes everything that follows: legislative control almost never changes. Across the five composition snapshots, nearly every chamber stays on the same side of the 50-percent line. In eleven years, exactly one flips.

That matters for the method. A two-way fixed-effects panel (state and year effects, errors clustered by state) gives the headline estimate, but with the key variable barely moving within states, a significant coefficient can be a difference between states wearing the costume of a within-state effect. To guard against that, restriction is also treated as a prediction problem and tested with leave-one-state-out cross-validation, and an L1 (Lasso) model is asked to choose between party control and majority size.

+ The rest of the evidence

The headline regression coefficient on seat margin is positive and significant. On its own that looks like a dial. But it is identified almost entirely off the Republican end of the range, and three checks show it does not hold up.

Check 1 — split by who controlsEstimating the slope separately within Republican- and Democratic-controlled chambers gives a positive number on both sides, and the two are not statistically distinguishable. The data cannot actually locate a size effect on either side.

Check 2 — find where restriction happensLine states up by how Republican their legislature is and waiver use appears to rise with the margin. But it comes from three states, and one of them (Indiana, which ran a single waiver program across the whole period) carries it. Remove Indiana and the gradient reverses.

Check 3 — placebosIn samples where there is no expansion population to restrict, the margin has no effect, while the main estimates sit well away from zero. The pattern is specific to the states and years where restriction is actually possible.

Finally, an L1 (Lasso) model handed both party control and seat margin keeps control and shrinks the margin to zero. Every test points the same way: party direction matters, majority size does not.

+ What this does not claim

One party-control flip occurs in eleven years, so the panel is underpowered to resolve the exact shape just past the threshold. The claim is that no size effect is detectable here, not that it is exactly zero. A setting with more turnover in legislative control could still reveal a faint one.
